01

Context

The problem behind the product not just screens.

Rosterix is the system a large shift-based workforce runs on: project managers tag employees to projects, build monthly rosters, calculate shift allowances, and push the numbers to payroll - all under a compliance-heavy approval chain that runs from Line Manager to HR Business Partner to Regional Head.

The legacy version worked, in the way spreadsheets next to a database "work." Rosters were rebuilt from memory every month. Rule violations - an employee scheduled six days straight, a double-tagging conflict - surfaced only after a save was rejected, or worse, after payroll had already queried it. Every persona in the chain was making decisions with less context than the person before them had.

Rosterix 2.0 had two mandates that pulled in different directions: modernize a genuinely complex enterprise workflow, and embed an AI layer across nine use cases - automated rostering, conflict detection, approval-risk prediction, anomaly detection - without eroding the audit rigor that makes the tool enterprise-grade in the first place. My job as UX Lead was to make sure those two mandates reinforced each other instead of fighting.

"Every escalation level was deciding with less first-hand context than the level before it."
— Synthesized finding, cross-persona research, Section 5.7

Theme 01

Context arrives too late

Every level of approval - LM, HRBP, Regional Head - acts on a summary of a summary, with less first-hand data than the level before it.

Theme 02

Institutional memory lives in people

Pattern recognition - which requesters need scrutiny, which rosters repeat monthly - is undocumented and doesn't transfer to new staff.

Theme 03

Problems surface downstream

Conflicts and exceptions are caught by a payroll rejection two weeks later, not flagged at the moment the risky change is made.

Theme 04

Trust is earned in small steps

Every persona, regardless of seniority, wanted to see and approve an AI suggestion before it took effect - never have it applied silently.

03

Personas

Four archetypes, five roles people before pixels

HRBP and Regional Head share a decision pattern - approve on incomplete context, under real time pressure - so they consolidate into one working persona. Four archetypes carried through every subsequent design decision.

PP
The Portfolio Planner
Represents Project & Line Managers
Goal

Publish an accurate, compliant roster with minimal rework, and never get a payroll query three weeks after the fact.

Frustration

Rebuilds recognizable shift patterns from memory every single cycle; finds out about rule violations only after saving.

Needs from AI

A reviewable draft, never a silent decision - and a visible reason behind every suggestion.

"Show me the draft, tell me why, let me fix the two that are wrong. Don't just do it and tell me after."
SE
The Second Pair of Eyes
Represents HR Business Partners & Regional Heads
Goal

Approve confidently and quickly without missing something that becomes a compliance or cost problem later.

Frustration

Every request looks equally routine in the queue; final approvers act on the least first-hand context in the whole chain.

Needs from AI

Risk-prioritized queues and pattern history surfaced automatically, not held in one person's memory.

"An exception with no explanation and one with a documented reason take me the same five minutes. That's backwards."
OG
The Org-Wide Guardian
Represents HRSS Administrators
Goal

Keep organisation-wide data clean, and stop a change in one place from silently breaking something elsewhere.

Frustration

Conflicts surface downstream at payroll instead of at the moment of change; dense grids for six hours a day cause real fatigue.

Needs from AI

A before/after impact preview before committing a change, and one org-wide anomaly view instead of per-project checks.

"One backdated change can ripple into three other records I don't find out about for two weeks. Show me the ripple first."
IE
The Informed Employee
Represents tagged shift employees
Goal

Know their schedule with enough lead time to plan a life around it, and trust that their allowance is calculated correctly.

Frustration

Finds out about a roster change on arrival; has no self-service way to understand or question an allowance amount.

Needs from AI

Proactive, plain-language notice - not conversation, just visibility, delivered early enough to act on.

"I found out my shift changed because I got to the office and it didn't match what I remembered."

10

Reflection

As UX Lead

The hardest design problem on Rosterix was never a screen - it was a relationship. Every persona I interviewed had a different job, a different level of seniority, and a completely consistent answer to the same underlying question: I'll use the AI if I can see why, and only if I still get the final word.

That consistency is what let a small pattern set - the Explainability Layer, the confidence indicator, the non-blocking violation - carry across nine very different AI use cases without the product feeling like nine different products stitched together. The v1-to-v3 iteration on the roster assistant is the clearest evidence of that: the fix was never a better model, it was a better answer to "why."

Partnering with the ML engineers changed how I brief AI features now. Confidence scores, feature importance, and override logging aren't a v2 nice-to-have layered on top of a finished interaction - they're inputs to the interaction design from the first sketch, or the trust problem research surfaced never actually gets solved.

What I'd do differently with more runway: field the real moderated interviews the research report explicitly flagged as a gap, before more screens ship - the simulated panel was enough to unblock design, not enough to replace ground truth.

Next

Run 5–8 moderated interviews per persona to validate the pain-point matrix against real users.

Next

Usability-test the AI Assistant Panel specifically for trust calibration - does a shown confidence score change behaviour the way the research predicts?

Next
